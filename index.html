The Wall: fifty-six pigments through time

1503

On the fourth floor of the Harvard Art Museums, behind the glass walls of the Straus Center, stand more than 2,700 samples of raw colour: the Forbes Pigment Collection. Edward Forbes, director of the Fogg Museum from 1909, began it so conservators could compare a painting's materials against known samples. This wall is a small homage to it. Each jar is a pigment, shelved by the era it first appears. Move the dial and watch which jars a painter could actually fill.

            The Lab

            Conservation scientists date paintings partly by what is in them. If a picture contains a pigment that was invented later than the date it claims, it cannot be what it says it is. Archaeologists call this the terminus post quem: the earliest a thing could have been made.

            How X-ray fluorescence works

            An XRF spectrometer shines X-rays at the paint. They knock electrons out of the inner shells of atoms; as other electrons fall in to fill the gaps, each element releases a second, weaker X-ray at its own fixed energy. The instrument counts photons by energy, so each peak names an element. Lead in lead white, mercury in vermilion and titanium in titanium white all have fingerprints you can read.

            XRF has limits, and the cases use them. It reports elements, not compounds, so copper could be 1,000-year-old azurite or 1935 phthalocyanine blue. Light elements and organic pigments such as indigo, madder or modern synthetics are nearly invisible to it. Some peaks overlap: arsenic and lead sit almost on top of each other. Real labs add Raman spectroscopy, microscopy and chromatography; their findings appear in each case's lab notes. The spectra here are simulated at the true emission energies, but the peak heights are illustrative.
